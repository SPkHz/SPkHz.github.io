Measuring Polarization Dependent Loss (PDL) in Silicon Photonic Waveguides

All-States polarization sweep on a silicon photonic waveguide chip (1550 nm), with and without a fiber amplifier.

Course: EE-548 Silicon Photonics
Lab: 02 (dated 2025-09-11)
Topic: Polarization Dependent Loss (PDL) using the All-States method

Overview

Polarization Dependent Loss (PDL) is the variation in insertion loss with polarization state for a device under test (DUT). In silicon photonics, PDL commonly comes from waveguide and coupler asymmetry, stress/birefringence, and polarization-dependent coupling.

This lab measures the DUT’s PDL by sweeping many polarization states and recording transmitted power.

Two setups were compared:

  • Setup A: All-States method (no amplifier)
  • Setup B: All-States method with a fiber amplifier to keep measurements within the power meter’s dynamic range

Background

Definition: PDL

Let the transmitted power during a polarization sweep reach a maximum and minimum:

[ \mathrm{PDL\ (dB)} = P{\max}(\mathrm{dBm}) - P{\min}(\mathrm{dBm}) ]

This works because dBm is already logarithmic (a difference in dBm is a ratio in linear power).

Why the amplifier matters

Large total loss (insertion loss + PDL excursions) can push the detected signal toward the noise floor and/or outside the meter’s calibrated range. Adding an amplifier does not change the DUT’s PDL—it simply shifts the received power upward so that both maxima and minima are measurable.

Experimental setup

Setup A measures the DUT directly. Setup B inserts a fiber amplifier to keep the received signal inside the power meter’s usable dynamic range.
The polarization controller was manually swept to cover many states (broad coverage on the Poincaré sphere). High DOP indicates a well-defined polarization state during the sweep.

Raw instrument captures

The received power varies strongly with polarization state, indicating significant PDL. With the amplifier enabled (Setup B), the trace is shifted upward and remains within the meter’s readable range.

Results (digitized from screenshots)

Because the power meter screenshots are continuous traces (not exported CSV), the curves were digitized in Python and re-plotted to extract (P{\max}), (P{\min}), and PDL.

Discussion

What changes between setups?

  • Setup A (no amp): The received power can dip close to the meter’s lower measurable range, increasing uncertainty in (P_{\min}).
  • Setup B (with amp): The amplifier shifts the entire trace upward, improving confidence in both maxima and minima and reducing the chance of clipping or flooring.

Why PDL can be large in silicon photonics

Even nominally “single-mode” silicon waveguides can show polarization sensitivity because:

  • TE and TM modes see different effective indices and confinement.
  • Asymmetric cross-sections and sidewall roughness create birefringence and differential scattering.
  • Couplers (grating couplers, edge couplers, tapers) can be strongly polarization selective.

Post-lab questions

1) What physical asymmetries give rise to PDL?
Geometric asymmetry (waveguide cross-section, bends, tapers), fabrication non-uniformity (sidewall roughness), stress-induced birefringence, and polarization-selective coupling structures (especially grating couplers).

2) Why can insertion loss + PDL push the signal below detection?
Insertion loss reduces the baseline power; PDL adds additional polarization-dependent attenuation. The combined worst-case loss can push (P_{\min}) near the detector noise floor or below the meter’s specified range.

3) How does a fiber amplifier affect measurement and reliability?
It boosts the optical power so the power meter reads within a higher-confidence region (better SNR, less quantization/threshold effects). The main tradeoffs are added ASE noise, potential saturation, and the need to keep the amplifier operating in a linear region.

4) Why is dynamic range important in photonics experiments?
If the instrument cannot accurately measure the full range of the signal (especially minima), then computed metrics like PDL become biased. Understanding dynamic range prevents “hidden clipping” at the top and “flooring” at the bottom.
